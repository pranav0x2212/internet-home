## A 17 Year Old Kid

When I first walked into college, engineering wasn't a dream of mine. I had chosen Electronics and Communication Engineering because I had some time tinkering with electronics before thanks to my uncle,
but beyond that I had very little idea what engineering actually meant. I wasn't someone with a grand roadmap, a list of goals, or a vision of where I wanted to be in four years (oh and I certainly didn't have 
the long, luscious wavy hair I am well known for). 

Like most 17 year olds, I took life lightly. I'd attend classes, come home, play games and repeat. I wasn't particularly worried about internships, research, higher studies or careers. I just assumed I'd figure
things out as I went along. Looking back now, its funny at how little I knew: not just about engineering, but about people around me, friendships, failure, curiosity and even myself. 

This isn't the story of how college made me successful. Heck, I am far from it. It's rather the story of how countless ordinary moments, conversations, projects, mistakes and people slowly changed the way 
I looked at the world.

---

## The People

It's funny how a place slowly becomes familiar, not because you've memorized every corridor, but because you've associated every corner with someone.

> _Each of us meets a number of people in our life, and some of them we forget, and some we remember._  
> — Agatha Christie (from The Murder of Roger Ackroyd)

When college began, almost everyone around me was a stranger. The first few weeks were spent exploring the main campus and trying to remember the faces I'd met only once before. Somewhere in that chaos, people slowly 
started becoming familiar. 

Some of the earliest people I met weren't even from my department/campus. They were seniors and students from other branches who made college feel a little less intimidating, giving pointers so we wouldn't repeat the same 
mistakes they had made. Around the same time, I also met [Anshul](https://anshulparuchuri.netlify.app/). We were in the same batch, and although neither of us knew it then, we'd spend the next three years watching each other grow
in our own, beautiful ways. 

Most of my friends during first year happened to be from Computer Science, so meeting another ECE student was comforting. That's how I met [Manjari](https://www.linkedin.com/in/manjari-kulkarni-8689a72a5/) and spoiler alert, you
will see her name pop up more often than you'd think in this post. Around the same time, I also met Lalith, who happened to be the person sitting next to me during our introductory class. Neither of us knew that we'd end up 
working together on more projects than I can count.

College was also the first time I had to travel across the city by myself every day. The commute became a routine, but it would've been a lot less enjoyable without [Kathit](https://kathit-joshi.vercel.app/), who lived in the same 
locality as me. Even today, most of our conversations are either about what we're working on or the newest food place he's discovered. 

By the end of first year, a few more people had quietly became part of my life. Krithika and [Nathan](https://polarhive.net/) were among them. At that time, each one of them were just simply classmates and friends. Looking back now, each of them would  come to 
occupy a different place in my college journey. Some pushed me to grow, some became people I could always count on, and some simply made those four years a lot more memorable.

## A New Beginning

Second year felt like a fresh start. I had started to grow my hair out after getting inspired by another senior who is also named Pranav (it's like Spider Society, we are everywhere). The pooled classrooms were gone and for the first 
time, I was surrounded entirely by ECE students. They'd continue to be with me for the next 2 years, work with me on various assignments, complain about submissions and slowly form bonds over the upcoming semesters.

Lalith and a few other classmates from first year were the only familiar faces. I still am not taking things seriously, but I somehow ended up doing way better in academics during my third semester of college. This was also the 
start of my interests in Digital Design and Computer Architecture. But I wouldn't act on my interests until much later in the story. 

Somewhere along the way, talking to Manjari became part of my daily routine. The only free time she usually had was after 11 PM, precisely when I headed to bed. So I found myself staying awake most nights just so we could share how each other's
day went by. We shared similar interests in music and art, both loved dosa, and for reasons I'll never understand, she thoroughly enjoyed bullying me. 

Whether it was something exciting or something that had gone terribly wrong, she somehow became one of the first people to hear about it. Looking back, I don't remember what we talked about most days. I just remmeber that we talked.

![drawing](https://i.ibb.co/4nmN3zty/drawing.jpg)

> One of my favorite pieces by Manjari. We planned to post our respective artworks at the same time and this is a memorable one.

On the other side of things, life was beginning to change in a different way. Around this time, Nathan became someone who quietly influenced the way I approached learning. He was one of those people who always seemed to be a step ahead, 
happily running Linux on his dual booted machine while the rest of us were terrified of breaking our Windows installations. I can't recall how many people he helped install Linux for the first time, patiently walking everyone through the process
and answering the same set of questions over and over again.

I wasn't any different. I asked plenty of questions, many of them probably obvious in hindsight, but he never made me feel like they were stupid. Through [HSP](https://hsp-ec.xyz/), he introduced me to a lot more than just Linux and FOSS.
Watching him work, I learnt how to reason from first principles, document what I built, [asking questions the right way](https://dontasktoask.com/), and share my work out there regardless of how small it may seem. I don't think he gave me motivation as much
as he gave me direction. Once I had that, curiosity carried the rest.

## The Rabbit Hole

Fourth semester was where everything started to fall apart, or perhaps, come together. I had slowly stopped caring about the subjects that filled my timetable, not because engineering had become boring, but because I had discovered parts of it that 
felt much more exciting. While lectures talked about one thing, I found myself spending evenings chasing something entirely different. 

It started with understanding Linux enough to use it for normal day to day use. I remember my first distro being [Pop!_OS](https://system76.com/pop) and the more I used it, the more frustrated I became with Windows. Not that Windows was unusable, but Linux
had shown me a different way of interacting with my computer. It provided total freedom, I had control over changing any part of how it looks and works. I didn't have to schedule a software update every other week and more importantly, it made me curious.
It became a tool that I genuinely wanted to understand underneath the hood. 

![popOS](https://i.ibb.co/Wpnv3sxK/image.png)

> My very first Pop!_OS setup. I took this screenshot on 18 October 2024, after fully nuking Windows and having almost 500 GB just for messing with Linux.

As I explored more, I had started to discover areas that were super fascinating (Digital Design, Computer Architecture, RISC-V, FPGA development, SoC Design and Cryptography). One question almost always led to another. A blog would point me towards another one, 
which would lead me to a random 10 year old Github repository, and at the end of the day I would have read about an entirely different but interesting topic. I conciously chose to spend more time exploring these interests than preparing for 
exams, and unsuprisingly, my grades reflected that. Fourth semester ended up being the lowest GPA I scored throughout my college life. I don't regret making that decision as it helped me discover what kind of engineer I want to become. 

Summer vacations arrived soon after, and for the first time, I felt confident enough to mentor juniors as part of HSP's annual mentorship program, [Tilde](https://hsp-ec.xyz/announcements/tilde-4.0). Anshul and I each mentored different teams, helping these students 
build some of their first projects. I went into the program thinking I'd be the one teaching others, but I quickly realized I was learning just as much myself. Explaining concepts forced me to understand them more deeply. I also learnt to adapt my explanations so that
every mentee, regardless of their background, could stay on the same page. More importantly, I realized that sometimes all a person needs is a gentle push to begin their own journey, much like mine. Proudly enough, it was the first hardware project to be successfully completed at Tilde :D

Outside that, I was trying to overcome another hurdle. I had started writing blogs, pushing projects to my Github and coming across interesting papers, but almost nobody knew about any of it. I was anxious about sharing my work in front of ninety people, 
convinced that whatever I posted would either be ignored or simply disappear among the hundreds of messages. So I started small, shared a blog here, a Github repository  there, a paper whenever I found one and just mingling with the abundant number of seniors helped me lose that anxiety slowly.

Ironically, I wasn't even part of HSP's technical discussion group yet. I had joined as a social media volunteer, while quietly spending most of my free time exploring the technical side on my own. I think HSP naturally attracted a certain kind of person. Many of them 
were outliers, not because they scored the highest grades, but because they simply loved building things. Around them, sharing a milestone never felt like bragging. It simply became another reason for everyone else to keep building.

## Finding My Place

By the time fifth semester arrived, curiosity had stopped being just another hobby. The countless evenings where I spent falling down one rabbit hole after another had slowly begun to show results. One of my biggest milestones during this time was 
understanding and designing my own [RISC-V processor](https://github.com/pranav0x2212/RISCape), followed shortly by a minimal [System-on-Chip using Bluespec](https://github.com/pranav0x2212/Aetheron). The designs themselves weren't particularly groundbreaking, 
but they represented much bigger to me. First, of course it had taught me way beyond any college course could have ever done. But it sparked discussions with seniors, brought in valuable feedback, and only pushed me further down the rabbit hole.

With that confidence, I slowly started taking on more responsibility around college as well. I became the head of the Electronics club, hoping to organize events and hackathons that would get more students interested in hardware. College bureaucracy ensured 
most of those ideas never really made it out the group chat. It was frustrating, but I suppose every student club eventually discovers that enthusiasm alone isn't always enough.

One of my favorite memories that semester came through an Embedded Systems project. My team consisted of [Anjali](https://www.linkedin.com/in/anjali-sreekrishnan-0b0703291/) and Karen, and we called ourselves `Labubu geng`. Do not judge me based on the name, dear reader, it's just another proof I'm a Genz kid. 
Our project was to build a simple Obstacle Detection Robot and it was probably one of the most intense 2 weeks of college trying to get that silly robot working. Sitting around in an empty college till 7 PM figuring out why the hardware refuses to work with your 
friends is another vibe of its own. I will always cherish those memories :)

Around this time, another lesson quietly found me. After months of reading and building about processors, I had unknowingly developed a bit of an ego. When my college department offered an additional RISC-V CPU Design session, I skipped it, convinced I already knew
enough and that it would be a waste of my time. My professor noticed this and called me out and I'm quite glad he did. I did know a fair bit for a student at that stage, but it was never really about having the knowledge. This just taught me that confidence and 
curiosity can coexist, but confidence without curiosity quickly becomes arrogance. 

Somewhere during all of this, I also stumbled upon [Tiny Tapeout](https://tinytapeout.com/), an open source silicon community. I submitted my [first design](https://prawns.dev/blogs/herald) and met people from all over the world who shared the same/more enthusiasm 
for hardware. The discussions in the community showed me just how welcoming and passionate the open source hardware world really is. More importantly, it made me realize that some of the most meaningful mentorship doesn't always happen inside a classroom. 
Looking back, I had no idea that submitting that tiny design would introduce me to people who would profoundly shape the next chapter of my journey.

## The Feedback Loop

When I first started this post, I thought it would mostly be about hardware, Linux, research and the project I built over my college life. Every meaningful milestone, be it the processors I built, the robot I struggled to bring to life or the countless Github repositories
I shared with my seniors, is something I'm incredibly proud of it. But they're only part of the story. Every single piece of my work somehow had another person standing quietly beside it.

Nathan introduced me to something far more than Linux. My seniors challenged my ideas, reviewed my work and were always willing to share their knowledge with me. My professors, sometimes knowingly and sometimes unknowingly, taught me lessons that extended far beyond lecture slides.
HSP became a place where I met people who were just as excited about building things as I was.

And of course, [Ananya Verma](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ananya-verma-6397b5321/) and Anjali, my two biggest hypemen. They somehow found a way to get excited about every tiny thing I built, even when I thought it wasn't worth talking about. 

Through Tiny Tapeout, I met people from around the world who somehow found time to mentor complete strangers on the internet. One of them was [Julia Desmazes](https://essenceia.github.io/), a retired CPU engineer from France. We had never met in person, yet she constantly encouraged my work and 
even recommended me on her personal website. It was quite a surprise for me when I found it, but it was just a quiet investment in my future from someone who believed in me, even though she had only known me for a very short period of time. I hope to eat waffles with her some day!

![chat](https://i.ibb.co/ch15dvtQ/ooo.png)

> She really loves waffles and coffee. And I love dosa and coffee.

But if there are people who deserve the most credit for everything I've managed to do, it's my family. It's not easy to raise your own sister's kids after her demise, especially along with other problems life throws at you. Life has been held together by my grandparents, my aunts and my uncles,
each supporting me in their own way. Some made sure I grew up disciplined and responsible. Some patiently listened to me ramble about processors and computer architecture despite understanding very little of what I was talking about. My grandmother still worries whether I've eaten on time before asking about anything else. 
And if you ask me, I'm fairly certain she'd defend me no matter what trouble I got myself into. 

![family](https://i.ibb.co/NdQJW9Fv/Prawns-2026-07-26-18-59-54.jpg)

> I'll always be their _Appu Kutta_ :)

As I write this, I'm just getting ready for whatever comes next. Maybe it's graduate school, research or a corporate job or something I haven't even imagined yet. I still don't have every answer, and honestly, I don't think I ever will.
What I do know is that a seventeen year old kid who entered engineering with no real roadmap finally found his place. Not because he was the smartest person in the room, but because he was surrounded by people who shared what they knew and believed in him.

Looking back, maybe that's what these four years were really about. Every conversation, every piece of advice and every opportunity someone gave me eventually found its way back into something I built or shared with someone else. Maybe that's the real feedback loop!
